\abstract{Large-language-model (LLM) assistants frequently stream responses token by token using Server-Sent Events (SSE). Observable frame sizes can correlate with token lengths and thereby leak information about the generated text. This paper reports a \emph{controlled} cross-model evaluation of output reconstruction from \emph{idealized} one-token-per-frame SSE length traces across seven open-weight language models (300 aligned prompts per model; 2100 samples). A shared Weiss-style T5 pipeline reconstructs text hypotheses, which are scored with a fixed term-frequency cosine similarity $\phi$ (lexical overlap, not paraphrase-aware semantic similarity). Mean $\phi$ ranges from $0.324$ for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.310,0.338]$) to $0.024$ for Phi-3.5-mini-instruct ($[0.020,0.028]$). Paired per-prompt Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction find 19 of 21 cross-model differences significant. A pilot defense evaluation on two models and 30 topic-balanced prompts per model examines length bucketing, fixed framing, two- and four-token batching, and randomized padding against the frozen, non-adaptive reconstructor. All ten defense--baseline comparisons remain significant after Holm correction; observed leakage reductions range from 35.0\% to 90.3\%, and no defended sample exceeds $\phi>0.5$ (Wilson upper bound 11.35\% per cell). These results quantify uncertainty and demonstrate mitigation potential, but do not establish robustness against adaptive attackers or an end-to-end TLS traffic attack.}

\section{Introduction}
Interactive LLM assistants commonly return answers incrementally to reduce perceived latency. A frequent implementation pattern is token streaming over Server-Sent Events (SSE), in which each emitted chunk is encapsulated in a text frame transmitted over an encrypted transport. While encryption protects payload content, it does not necessarily eliminate metadata leakage: observable sizes can correlate with token lengths and thereby reveal information about the generated response~\cite{weiss2024prompt}.

Weiss \emph{et al.} demonstrated token-length side-channel reconstruction on real browser and API traffic, recovering 27\% of responses and inferring the topic for 53\% under their criteria~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. Concurrent work studies merged/batched API outputs~\cite{li2026length} and related streaming metadata attacks~\cite{whisperleak2025}. This paper asks a complementary question: under a controlled, idealized one-token-per-frame observation model, how does reconstruction quality vary across open-weight models when prompts, decoding parameters, reconstructor, and semantic metrics are held fixed?

\textbf{Scope.} We study an \emph{ideal token-length oracle} from an instrumented SSE harness, not a full remote attack on ciphertext records. An on-path observer of TLS/HTTP/2/QUIC traffic typically sees records that may coalesce or fragment SSE frames, add variable overhead, or pass through proxies. Framing the study as a controlled benchmark isolates model-dependent length patterns from transport effects; end-to-end remote extraction is future work.

The contribution of this paper is fourfold. First, we describe a controlled methodology for collecting idealized per-token length traces and reconstructing text from them. Second, we report a comparative evaluation across seven open-weight models with a single fixed scoring backend and uncertainty-aware statistical inference. Third, we report response-length covariates and interpret the ranking as reconstructor transfer rather than intrinsic ``leakiness.'' Fourth, we evaluate five defense configurations against a frozen attacker and quantify both leakage reduction and bandwidth/frame-count costs.

\section{Related Work}
Side-channel attacks infer sensitive information from indirect observables such as timing, size, power, or electromagnetic emanations~\cite{kocher1996timing}. Network-observable metadata has long been shown to be dangerous~\cite{song2001timing,chen2010sidechannel}. Weiss \emph{et al.} introduced token-length side-channel reconstruction against AI assistants, including known-plaintext fine-tuning to the victim's writing style, and evaluated the attack on live ChatGPT and Copilot traffic~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. Li \emph{et al.} study reconstruction when APIs merge multiple tokens into single packets~\cite{li2026length}, raising the novelty bar for length-channel work targeting realistic deployments. Related analyses of streaming metadata further demonstrate that encryption alone is insufficient~\cite{whisperleak2025}. Our contribution is a controlled multi-model benchmark under idealized one-token-per-frame traces rather than a new end-to-end remote exploit.

The reconstruction pipeline uses T5-style text-to-text transformers~\cite{raffel2020exploring}. Evaluation uses normalized Levenshtein distance~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}, ROUGE~\cite{lin2004rouge}, and a fixed term-frequency cosine similarity $\phi$ (cf.\ Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019sentence}).

\section{Materials and Methods}
\subsection{Threat Model and Experimental Scope}
We assume an evaluation pipeline that obtains a sequence of per-token UTF-8 lengths and attempts to reconstruct the assistant response. In our harness, those lengths are derived from SSE frames of the form \texttt{data: <token>\textbackslash n\textbackslash n} via $L^{\mathrm{tok}}_t = L^{\mathrm{frame}}_t - 8$, discarding the terminal \texttt{[DONE]} frame. The reconstruction stage is fed length sequences only.

This matches the token-length channel abstraction of Weiss \emph{et al.}~\cite{weiss2024prompt}, but measurements come from an instrumented server that \emph{enforces} one token per SSE frame. The experiment is therefore a controlled reconstruction study under an ideal length oracle. A network adversary would still need to extract comparable lengths from ciphertext records that may merge/split frames, add variable overhead, or carry JSON wrappers---conditions we do not claim to have reproduced.

\subsection{Streaming Harness and Trace Collection}
The harness implements SSE streaming in a strict one-token-per-frame configuration. For every emitted token it logs token text, token UTF-8 length, SSE frame UTF-8 length, and relative emission timestamp. Token text is logged for auditing and offline metric recomputation; the attack itself uses lengths only.
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{tok}}_t = L^{\mathrm{frame}}_t - 8,
\end{equation}
where 8 bytes are the fixed SSE overhead (\texttt{data:\ } and \texttt{\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}).

\subsection{Reconstruction Pipeline}
The inversion pipeline follows a Weiss-style procedure: heuristic segmentation into sentence-like chunks; first-chunk decoding with \texttt{royweiss1/T5\_FirstSentences}; subsequent chunks with \texttt{royweiss1/T5\_MiddleSentences}; candidate ranking by the sum of transition log-probabilities (not length-normalized). We use the \emph{same} public reconstructor checkpoints for every victim and do \emph{not} fine-tune on victim-style known plaintext. Cross-model differences therefore measure \emph{transfer} of this fixed reconstructor onto each model's length traces (tokenizer, whitespace, style, refusals, length distributions), not intrinsic leakage after per-model adaptation~\cite{weiss2024prompt}.

\subsection{Models, Prompt Corpus, and Protocol}
We evaluate seven open-weight language models: six instruction/chat-tuned models (Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Gemma-2-2B-it, TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct) and one base model (Phi-1.5), which is not instruction-tuned or RLHF-aligned according to its model card.

The prompt corpus comprises 15 topical files with 20 prompts each (300 prompts). The same prompts are issued to every model in the same order. Victim decoding is deterministic (\texttt{temperature}$=0.0$, \texttt{top\_p}$=1.0$). The standardized subset fixes $N=300$, \texttt{max\_new\_tokens}$=96$, \texttt{samples\_per\_segment}$=3$, \texttt{max\_sentences}$=3$, and \texttt{num\_first\_candidates}$=3$. A shared token budget does not imply comparable amounts of text across tokenizers.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Reconstruction quality uses normalized Levenshtein distance $ed_{\mathrm{norm}}$ ($\downarrow$), ROUGE-1/ROUGE-L precision ($\uparrow$), and cosine semantic similarity $\phi$ ($\uparrow$). We report the semantic recovery rate $\mathrm{SR}@0.5$ ($\uparrow$) as the proportion of samples with $\phi>0.5$ (avoiding the ambiguous acronym ASR). The threshold $0.5$ is a conventional operating point, not a human-calibrated criterion.

A prior mixed campaign computed $\phi$ with Sentence-BERT when available and TF cosine otherwise. We recompute $\phi$ for all 2100 samples with one fixed term-frequency cosine backend so that rankings use a comparable scale.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
The prompt index provides a natural pairing across victim models because every model receives the same 300 prompts. For each mean metric we compute a percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence interval using $B=10{,}000$ resamples and seed \texttt{20260706}. Uncertainty for $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$ is reported with a two-sided Wilson 95\% interval. Cross-model differences in per-prompt $\phi$ are tested with two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Family-wise error over the $\binom{7}{2}=21$ comparisons is controlled with the Holm--Bonferroni procedure at $\alpha=0.05$; rank-biserial correlation is reported as effect size. The same paired procedure is used for ten defense--baseline comparisons, with Holm correction over that family. These tests assess the fixed benchmark population and do not remove confounding by tokenizer or output style~\cite{efron1994bootstrap,wilcoxon1945individual,holm1979simple}.

\subsection{Defense Configurations}
The defense pilot uses 30 topic-balanced prompts for each of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Five transformations are applied to the observable trace: rounding token-frame lengths to the next 8-byte bucket (\texttt{bucket\_8}); online fixed-width 32-byte framing (\texttt{pad\_32}); grouping two or four consecutive tokens into one observable frame (\texttt{batch\_2}, \texttt{batch\_4}); and seeded uniform random padding of 0--8 bytes (\texttt{rand\_pad\_8}). The reconstructor is frozen and is not retrained on defended traces. Effectiveness is expressed as $100(1-\bar\phi_d/\bar\phi_0)$, while utility cost is summarized by mean byte overhead and mean observable frame count.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Control Covariates}
Under \texttt{max\_new\_tokens}$=96$, most models truncate at or near the budget (truncation often $95$--$100\%$). Mean UTF-8 bytes per token differ substantially across models (approximately $5.0$ for Qwen2.5 versus approximately $3.5$ for TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini). Equal token counts therefore do not imply equal textual content; these covariates confound any cross-model ranking of reconstruction quality.

\subsection{Cross-Model Comparison under Standardized Conditions}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the primary comparable subset with recomputed $\phi$. Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct achieves the highest mean semantic similarity ($\phi=0.3242$), followed by Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct ($\phi=0.3096$) and Phi-1.5 ($\phi=0.2747$ under the fixed backend). Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct shows intermediate reconstructability ($\phi=0.2538$), Gemma-2-2B-it $\phi=0.1798$, TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat $\phi=0.0428$, and Phi-3.5-mini-instruct the lowest ($\phi=0.0239$). \subsection{Inferential Comparison}
Nineteen of the 21 paired model comparisons remain significant after Holm correction. The two exceptions are Qwen2.5-1.5B versus Qwen2.5-3B (mean paired difference $0.0146$, bootstrap 95\% CI $[-0.0026,0.0314]$, rank-biserial $r=0.089$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.1838$) and Llama-3.2-3B versus the non-instruction-tuned Phi-1.5 (difference $-0.0209$, CI $[-0.0375,-0.0043]$, $r=-0.148$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0533$). Representative large contrasts include Qwen2.5-1.5B versus Phi-3.5-mini (difference $0.3002$, $r=1.000$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<0.0001$) and Llama-3.2-3B versus Gemma-2-2B (difference $0.0739$, $r=0.566$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<0.0001$). Thus, most observed differences are not explained by sampling variability over this aligned prompt set, although the tests do not establish causal model effects.

\subsection{Additional Evaluation Models}
Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Gemma-2-2B-it, and Phi-3.5-mini-instruct were included as additional evaluation models spanning distinct families. Under fully completed 300/300 runs, relative reconstructability among them is Llama-3.2-3B $>$ Gemma-2-2B $>$ Phi-3.5-mini-instruct.

\subsection{Effectiveness of Defensive Mechanisms}\label{sec:defense}
Table~\ref{tab:defenses} reports the defense pilot. Every defense reduces mean $\phi$ significantly relative to its paired baseline after Holm correction ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\leq0.000589$). Bucketing produces the strongest consistently low residual leakage (83.4--90.3\% reduction) at 82.4--88.6\% mean byte overhead. Batching reduces leakage by 67.7--69.2\% while halving or quartering the observable frame count and adding no payload-byte-sum overhead under this accounting. Randomized padding reduces leakage by 61.0--65.7\%. Fixed 32-byte framing is least effective in the evaluated online implementation (35.0--36.8\%) and incurs 538--565\% byte overhead.

No defended sample exceeds $\phi>0.5$. This observation must not be read as zero risk: with $n=30$ per cell, the Wilson 95\% upper bound is 11.35\%. \section{Discussion}
\subsection{Interpretation of Reconstruction Differences}
Under idealized length traces and a shared reconstructor, reconstruction quality is strongly model dependent. A service that exposes token-granular streaming lengths can enable model-dependent recovery \emph{if} an adversary obtains those lengths. We do not claim a mechanistic explanation: tokenizer behaviour, output style, truncation under a shared budget, and reconstructor training distribution are entangled.

\subsection{Reconstructor Transfer vs.\ Intrinsic Leakage}
The ranking should be read as transferability of \texttt{royweiss1} checkpoints onto each model's traces. Weiss \emph{et al.} improved reconstruction by fine-tuning to victim style~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. A stronger matrix---shared reconstructor, per-model fine-tuned reconstructors, and cross-model transfer---would separate leakiness of length patterns from compatibility with a particular inversion model.

\subsection{Limitations}
(1)~Idealized length oracle, not TLS record extraction. (2)~Deterministic victim decoding. (3)~Fixed public reconstructor without per-model or defense adaptation. (4)~TF cosine $\phi$ measures lexical overlap rather than semantic correctness. (5)~$\mathrm{LR}@0.5$ is conventional, not human-calibrated. (6)~High truncation under \texttt{max\_new\_tokens}$=96$. (7)~The defense pilot covers only two models and 30 prompts per cell; its byte accounting omits protocol headers, latency, and user-experience costs. Larger multi-seed trials and adaptive attackers are required before deployment claims.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
This work is defensive security research: quantify reconstructability under controlled conditions to motivate safer streaming. Live offensive tooling against third-party services is out of scope.

\section{Conclusions}
We presented a controlled cross-model evaluation of LLM output reconstruction from idealized SSE token-length traces. Bootstrap intervals and multiplicity-corrected paired tests show that most cross-model differences are statistically distinguishable on the aligned prompt corpus. All evaluated defenses significantly reduce leakage against the frozen reconstructor; 8-byte bucketing yields the largest consistent reduction, while batching offers the most favorable measured bandwidth trade-off. These findings are evidence of mitigation potential rather than proof of security. The most important next steps are live encrypted-traffic extraction, adaptive reconstruction on defended traces, human-calibrated semantic evaluation, and production-facing latency and bandwidth measurements.
