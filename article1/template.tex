\abstract{Streaming large language model (LLM) services can expose response-length metadata even when payloads are encrypted. We evaluate a fixed T5 reconstructor through 6600 reconstructions from validated application-layer length traces, selecting candidates by sampling-processed transition scores through the first end-of-sequence (EOS) token. For 300 aligned prompts per model, five archived-reference cohorts yield mean term-frequency cosine scores ($\phi$) of 0.509--0.577; two additional archived cohorts have malformed word boundaries. Separately collected TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini responses with verified detokenization yield means of 0.468 and 0.527. Legacy rendering of their saved token IDs reproduces all 600 corresponding archived references and length vectors. Changing both the trace and scoring reference increases mean $\phi$ by 0.384 and 0.397 under matched reconstruction settings. In two-model pilots, four of six partial-ablation contrasts remain significant after Holm correction; neither cross-prompt contrast is conclusive. Five framing transformations reduce frozen-reconstructor mean scores by 43.8--89.6\%. A five-seed study with a local empirical maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) length inverse recovers much of the loss under bucketing and batching. Reconstruction scopes and retained encoder coverage differ across these experiments. The findings quantify lexical behavior of an instrumented pipeline and demonstrate the importance of validating both observations and reference rendering. They do not measure semantic disclosure or demonstrate passive extraction from encrypted transport.}

\section{Introduction}
Interactive large language model (LLM) services stream partial responses to reduce latency. One implementation uses Server-Sent Events (SSE), with generated text carried in application events. Encryption hides the event contents but not necessarily the shape of the communication. If application-event boundaries or correlated record sizes are observable, a sequence of lengths can reveal properties of the underlying tokens.

This paper separates reconstruction from a known length trace from extraction of that trace from encrypted traffic. The laboratory observable is the ordered UTF-8 byte length of each token fragment emitted by an instrumented service, before transport processing. A validated log oracle supplies these lengths and preserves newline bytes and empty fragments. This abstraction removes segmentation by HTTP and Transport Layer Security (TLS), buffering, retransmission, and multiplexing from the experiment. Validation in Section~\ref{sec:measurement-audit} binds each input vector to its source log and scoring reference. All quantitative reconstruction results reported below come from the completed campaign with EOS-bounded candidate selection. No experiment here establishes passive extraction of event boundaries from encrypted traffic.

Building on Weiss \emph{et al.}, who reconstructed streamed responses from token-length patterns using a T5-based inversion pipeline~\cite{weiss2024prompt}, we examine one fixed reconstructor across open-weight victim models. Partial trace ablations assess its sensitivity to ordered lengths, and framing transformations test its response without retraining. The seven-model cohort retains archived emitted texts; TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini are diagnostic within that cohort because of malformed word boundaries. A separate two-model experiment uses previously collected responses with verified full-sequence detokenization, reconstructed in the same completed campaign.

The study addresses three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:},leftmargin=4.2em,labelwidth=3.2em,labelsep=0.6em]
    \item Do ordered logged lengths improve lexical scores over controls that shuffle lengths, replace them with a constant, or use another prompt's trace?
    \item How do scores from validated length traces differ across seven archived victim-model cohorts, and how do separate experiments with verified reference decoding inform the two diagnostic series?
    \item How do bucketing, fixed-width units, batching, and randomized padding affect the evaluated pipeline, protected-payload size, and observable-unit count, and how does a prompt-disjoint maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) inverse change its scores?
\end{enumerate}

The contribution is an evaluation with explicit input provenance: 2100 reconstructions from validated archived lengths, 600 reconstructions of separately collected responses with verified decoding, two-model pilots of partial trace ablations and five framing configurations, and a five-seed MAP-inverse evaluation. All 6600 reconstructions were executed in the completed campaign; no earlier reconstruction predictions enter the reported results. The pilots and MAP study use different reconstruction scopes, so their baselines are not interchangeable. Full input vectors, decoder inputs, random seeds, candidate sequences, transition scores, generated texts and independent metric checks are retained in the accompanying artifact package. The source project is hosted at \url{https://github.com/jackaugustyn/LLeakM}. Appendix~\ref{app:runs} identifies the completed experiment artifacts and the frozen victim-response inputs.

\section{Related Work}
Side-channel attacks infer sensitive information from indirect observables such as timing and message size. Classical timing attacks showed that implementation-level leakage can defeat cryptographic protection without recovering plaintext directly~\cite{kocher1996timing}. Encrypted network applications likewise leak through packet sizes and timing. Keystroke inference, web-application traffic analysis, and website fingerprinting demonstrate that encryption does not hide communication shape~\cite{song2001timing,chen2010sidechannel,cai2012touching,sirinam2018deep}.

Traffic-analysis defenses transform that shape through padding, morphing, batching, or cover traffic. Traffic morphing attempts to make one traffic class resemble another, while subsequent studies show that efficient countermeasures may retain exploitable structure or impose substantial overhead~\cite{wright2009morphing,dyer2012peekaboo}. For this reason, defense effectiveness should be evaluated by reporting both the leakage that remains and the resulting bandwidth and latency overhead, rather than by treating padding as either fully protective or ineffective.

For generative AI, Weiss \emph{et al.} introduced T5-based sequence-to-text inversion and, in their first-segment evaluation, reported 27\% of reconstructions above an embedding-cosine threshold of 0.9 and approximately 53\% above 0.5~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. Their cosine uses MiniLM sentence embeddings; its thresholds and first-segment scope are not directly comparable to our term-frequency cosine and full-response $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$. Li \emph{et al.} subsequently studied API outputs that merge several tokens into one observable unit and showed that aggregation does not remove the side channel~\cite{li2026length}. Whisper Leak instead infers prompt topics from packet-size and timing patterns in encrypted LLM traffic~\cite{whisperleak2025}. Those studies address stronger deployment-facing threat models. The present work narrows the observation layer to ask a different question: how a fixed reconstructor transfers across victim models once a token-granular length trace is available, and how simple transformations affect that same reconstructor.

The reconstruction pipeline uses T5-style text-to-text transformers~\cite{raffel2020exploring}. Evaluation combines a reference-normalized Levenshtein distance~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}, ROUGE precision~\cite{lin2004rouge}, and one fixed term-frequency cosine score $\phi$. These are lexical measures. They should not be read as semantic correctness or disclosure of a usable answer.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\subsection{Threat Model and Objective}
We evaluate a length-only reconstructor for an instrumented LLM service. The observation is the ordered byte length of each emitted token fragment; the attacker receives neither the generating prompt, token identities, nor victim-model internals. The evaluator uses instrumented logs to verify these lengths and to obtain reference text, but supplies only the lengths to the reconstructor. This application-layer oracle does not recover event boundaries from TLS, HTTP/2, or QUIC traffic. The archived-reference cohort and the verified-reference two-model cohort are kept separate because verified byte lengths do not themselves establish ordinary detokenization of historical outputs.

The objective is to characterize lexical reconstruction in this controlled apparatus and the response of a fixed reconstructor to length transformations. The experiments capture per-token traces, reconstruct text from length sequences, and compare the reconstructions with the stored emitted responses. Prompts are used to generate those responses and are not supplied to the reconstructor. Figure~\ref{fig:threat_model} shows the intended information flow.

\subsection{Information-Theoretic Interpretation}
Let $R$ denote the stored emitted response and let $X=(L_1,\ldots,L_T)$ denote its ordered token-payload length trace in the idealized laboratory model. When a defense is applied, $X$ is transformed into the observable trace $Z=g(X,N)$, where $N$ is independent transformation randomness. The attacker produces $\widehat{R}=A(Z,U)$ using independent decoder randomness $U$. Conditional on a fixed fitted attacker, these relationships form the following Markov chain:
\begin{equation}
R \longrightarrow X \longrightarrow Z \longrightarrow \widehat{R}.
\end{equation}
According to the data-processing inequality,
\begin{equation}
I(R;\widehat{R}) \leq I(R;Z) \leq I(R;X).
\end{equation}
Neither processing stage increases mutual information about $R$ under these assumptions. The empirical controls are partial trace ablations: they preserve response-dependent features such as trace length, the length histogram, or mean length. A score advantage over an ablation indicates sensitivity to the altered features, but does not isolate all trace information from the decoder's language prior. The lexical score $\phi$ does not estimate mutual information, and its reduction for a fixed attacker does not measure defended-channel capacity.

For a single-segment reconstruction, let $E(Z)$ denote the deterministic encoder input after inversion (if used), segmentation, serialization and truncation. Then the chain refines to $R\rightarrow X\rightarrow Z\rightarrow E(Z)\rightarrow\widehat R$, and $I(R;E(Z))\leq I(R;Z)$. The complete trace $Z$ may retain variation that never reaches the decoder. This distinction matters for \texttt{pad\_32}: the audited single-call encoder inputs are constant within each evaluated condition (Section~\ref{sec:defense}). Multi-segment decoding additionally uses the previously selected text as context, as specified below.

\subsection{Streaming Harness and Trace Collection}
The instrumented harness associates each generated token position with a text fragment and records its UTF-8 length, serialized-write length, and relative timestamp. The historical implementation decoded each token independently; the new-generation implementation uses contextual prefix decoding and verifies that the fragments concatenate exactly to the full decoded token sequence. The new harness completes token generation and contextual decoding before emitting the fragments; it validates payload lengths rather than the timing of incremental generation. Each write has the SSE-like serialization \texttt{data: \textless fragment\textgreater\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}, and termination uses \texttt{data: [DONE]\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}. Raw newlines inside fragment text are not escaped, so a write is not necessarily a valid single SSE data event. The present experiments use validated lengths from the instrumented logs, not a line-based parser.

For the logged serialization, the payload length satisfies
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{log}}_t = L^{\mathrm{write}}_t - 8,
\end{equation}
where the eight bytes are the prefix \texttt{data: } and suffix \texttt{\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}. This identity concerns the complete emitted byte string, including newline bytes inside the fragment. The terminal \texttt{[DONE]} write is excluded. Validation checks per-fragment byte counts, their order, concatenated reference text, total payload bytes, and sample/log identity. For the verified-reference streams the collector also checks the total received byte count. Exact vectors, source hashes, and reference text are saved with every reconstruction input. The primary, control, defense, and MAP experiments all use this explicit log-length representation.

\subsection{Measurement and Reference-Text Audit}\label{sec:measurement-audit}
Every reconstruction input is an explicit vector of UTF-8 payload lengths obtained from the retained generation log. Validation recomputes each fragment's byte length, checks its ordered position and verifies that the concatenated fragments equal the scoring reference. Source hashes and immutable sample inventories bind the vectors, references and recorded seeds to the completed campaign. The experiments do not infer lengths from line parsing or undocumented historical reconstruction inputs.

The primary cohort reconstructs all 2100 validated archived vectors with the EOS-bounded selector defined below. The two verified-reference cohorts and all control, defense and MAP conditions use the same pinned reconstruction implementation. The reconstructor is seeded after checkpoint loading so that cache initialization does not change a sample's random stream. The current campaign reuses frozen victim responses and length vectors; it does not regenerate victim responses.

Reference text requires a separate check. The historical harness's independent token decoding produced no ASCII spaces in 299/300 TinyLlama responses and 162/300 Phi-3.5-mini responses. Those 600 archived references remain diagnostic; validating their emitted byte lengths does not restore their word boundaries. Historical token IDs are unavailable for all seven models, so complete-sequence detokenization of the other archived series is also not certified.

To evaluate the affected models with ordinary reference text, a separate collection produced 300 responses per model from the same prompts using contextual detokenization. All 600 stored responses pass exact agreement between concatenated fragments and full-sequence decoding, with token IDs, tokenizer and checkpoint provenance retained; all contain ASCII spaces. They are reconstructed anew in the present campaign and reported as separate cohorts. Their collection checkpoint revisions, precision and placement are pinned; identity with the historical execution is not established.

Holding the separately recorded token IDs fixed, replay of the historical independent-token decoding reproduces every archived fragment, complete response and byte-length vector for all 300 TinyLlama and all 300 Phi-3.5-mini samples. Switching to complete-sequence decoding changes only ASCII word-boundary spaces in these records. The two renderings are reconstructed with the same T5 checkpoints, precision, device, settings and per-sample seeds. This matched comparison measures the joint effect of rendering on the length input and scoring reference. It does not separate those two contributions, recover unknown historical IDs or certify old checkpoint versions.

\subsection{Reconstruction Pipeline}\label{sec:reconstructor}
The inversion pipeline follows the sequence-to-text procedure introduced by Weiss \emph{et al.}~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. It first splits a length vector at observations equal to one and merges short chunks using a ten-unit minimum, with an additional list-boundary heuristic. These are heuristic segments rather than verified sentences. The first segment is decoded using \texttt{royweiss1/T5\_FirstSentences}; later segments use \texttt{royweiss1/T5\_MiddleSentences}, conditioned on the immediately preceding selected segment. T5 generation uses stochastic sampling with top-$k=50$, temperature 1 and a two-token no-repeat n-gram constraint. For candidate $c$, let $\ell_{c,t}$ be its normalized transition log-score after those sampling constraints, and let $\tau_c$ be the first generated EOS position, or the final returned position if EOS is absent. The selector uses
\begin{equation}
S(c)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_c}\ell_{c,t}.
\end{equation}
The EOS transition is included and all subsequent PAD positions are excluded. The score is not length-normalized. Among duplicate decoded texts, the highest score is retained; equal-score ties preserve candidate order. The selected text supplies the next segment's context.

These scores use the sampling-processed distributions, not the unconstrained model logits. Nonfinite scores before or at EOS cause validation failure; padding after EOS does not contribute to selection. Every generated candidate is retained for independent verification; calls with three candidates also retain their transition scores. All three-candidate conditions use this rule throughout the complete response, including subsequent context updates. The one-candidate MAP experiment bypasses candidate ranking. Appendix~\ref{app:selector} reports full-campaign validation.

The pinned model cards identify these checkpoints as the T5-Large fine-tunes used by Weiss \emph{et al.}, trained on the first answers from UltraChat's ``Questions About the World'' subset. The source procedure trains separate models for first segments and subsequent segments, with preceding-segment text providing context for the latter~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. We verify the downloaded checkpoint revisions but do not independently reproduce training or audit its underlying records. The original paper defines token lengths in characters; our traces measure UTF-8 bytes of emitted fragments. These units agree for ASCII text but can differ for non-ASCII text, adding a representation shift alongside differences in tokenizer and response style.

For defense experiments, the order is length transformation, optional MAP inversion, heuristic segmentation, serialization of each segment as underscore-prefixed integers, T5 tokenization, input truncation, and decoding. The encoder input is limited to 80 T5 tokens, including the instruction prefix and any preceding context; generation also uses \texttt{max\_length}=80. These limits are distinct from the victim's 96- or 512-token generation budget. One length value need not occupy one T5 token: for example, \texttt{\_32} occupies two tokens in the audited tokenizer. Changing the length values can therefore change both segmentation and the amount of trace retained by the encoder. Section~\ref{sec:robustness-methods} reports the audited coverage of the first segment in the MAP experiment.

The reconstruction design is conditional rather than purely tokenwise. Instead of recovering each token independently, it maps length patterns into coherent text spans and uses context to improve linguistic plausibility (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Our quantitative claims nevertheless concern lexical recovery; semantic correctness was not established by human annotation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Reconstruction quality was computed against the complete reference response $R$ using four lexical criteria. The normalized Levenshtein value is $\texttt{ed\_norm}=d(R,\widehat{R})/|R|$, using reference character length as the denominator; it can exceed 1 when the prediction is much longer than the reference~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}. ROUGE-1 precision and ROUGE-L precision measure unigram and longest-common-subsequence overlap~\cite{lin2004rouge}. The primary metric $\phi$ is cosine similarity between unweighted, case-normalized term-frequency vectors~\cite{salton1975vector}, with terms defined by \texttt{[A-Za-z0-9']+}. All reported metrics were recomputed from complete saved text, using independent edit-distance and subsequence implementations checked against dynamic programming. The MAP experiment scores the first heuristic segment's prediction against the entire response, not against an annotated first sentence.

The study also reports lexical recovery rate $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$, the proportion of samples with $\phi>0.5$. This threshold is a descriptive operating point, not a calibrated success criterion. Neither $\phi$ nor $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$ establishes factual, semantic, or operationally useful reconstruction.

\subsection{Trace-Information Controls}
We compared four inputs on the same topic-balanced set of 30 prompts for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct using direct logged lengths from the 96-token pilot: (i) the original trace; (ii) its lengths randomly permuted within the response; (iii) a constant sequence equal to its rounded mean length; and (iv) another prompt's trace cycled to the target trace's number of positions. These are partial trace ablations. Shuffling preserves the response's length histogram and number of positions, the constant condition preserves its mean-derived value and number of positions, and the cross-prompt condition preserves the target trace's number of positions while replacing the length pattern. Thus each condition retains some response-dependent information. The comparisons probe sensitivity to order, variation, and alignment; their residual scores cannot be assigned wholly to the language prior. The control experiment reconstructs up to three heuristic segments against the full stored response and saves the actual segment counts and encoder inputs. These comparisons are not estimates of mutual information.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
Because the same 300 prompts were issued to every model, comparisons of the retained scores used prompt-aligned observations. For the archived main and verified-reference cohorts, percentile-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals~\cite{efron1994bootstrap} use $B=10{,}000$ resamples from a generator initialized with seed \texttt{20260706}; the pinned aggregator consumes this generator in its recorded model, metric and contrast order. The pilot and MAP intervals use separately derived cell and contrast seeds, specified in Appendix~\ref{app:map-seeds}. Confidence intervals for mean pairwise differences were obtained by bootstrapping within-prompt differences. Wilson 95\% intervals were used for $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$. These nominal intervals describe variation across the corpus's prompt variants under prompt-level resampling. They neither account for dependence among the five variants of a scenario nor establish generalization to unseen scenarios, and they do not include repeated victim generations or unresolved measurement errors. A separate exploratory sensitivity analysis averages the five variants within each of the 60 scenarios, resamples those scenario means 10,000 times from the same aggregator's random-number stream, and applies paired signed-rank tests to the 60 aligned means with Holm correction over 21 pairs. It changes the resampling and testing unit while preserving the equal-weight corpus mean; it does not establish generalization to unseen scenarios or repair measurement error. Decoder-seed uncertainty is reported separately for a two-model subset (Section~\ref{sec:robustness-methods}).

Pairwise differences in per-prompt $\phi$ were assessed using two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}. Absolute nonzero differences receive average ranks for ties; zero differences are discarded. The normal approximation uses a tie-adjusted variance and a continuity correction of 0.5, with normal tails evaluated directly to avoid numerical cancellation. Holm--Bonferroni correction is applied separately to 21 model comparisons, six control--true-trace comparisons and ten defense--baseline comparisons~\cite{holm1979simple}; rank-biserial correlation summarizes effect size. Significance denotes adjusted $p<0.05$. A bootstrap interval for a mean difference and a multiplicity-adjusted signed-rank test address different summaries and are not treated as interchangeable.

\subsection{Defense Evaluation Protocol}
Five configurations were evaluated on the same 30 topic-balanced prompts for each of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, using direct logged lengths from earlier 96-token traces. These use different source responses and a lower victim-token budget than the primary cohort, with the same validated log-length representation. Let $\ell_t$ denote the logged token-payload byte length. The pilot reconstructs up to three heuristic segments, with three samples per segment, and scores their concatenation against the full stored response.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \texttt{bucket\_8}: replace $\ell_t$ by $8\lceil \ell_t/8\rceil$, independently and online for every event.
    \item \texttt{pad\_32}: replace each token-length observation by a constant 32-byte protected unit. The largest token in the evaluated subset occupied 16 UTF-8 bytes, so every token fit within the unit.
    \item \texttt{batch\_2} and \texttt{batch\_4}: sum the lengths of consecutive groups of two or four tokens into one attacker-visible value. The final incomplete group, if present, is retained.
    \item \texttt{rand\_pad\_8}: add an independently sampled integer $U_t\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{0,\ldots,8\}$ using the pilot seed recipe described below.
\end{itemize}
The transformed sequences were passed through segmentation, T5 tokenization, input truncation, and decoding. The reconstructor was frozen and was not retrained or calibrated on defended traces. We report recovery-score reduction as $100(1-\bar\phi_d/\bar\phi_0)$. Payload-size overhead compares the sum of transformed units with the sum of original token bytes. This deliberately narrow accounting excludes SSE syntax, transport headers, packetization, buffering delay, and user-perceived latency. Batching has zero overhead only under this payload-sum definition; its reduction in observable unit count is reported separately.

The control and defense experiments use explicit BLAKE2s-derived, condition-specific decoder seeds and record every seed and transformed vector. Shuffling uses seed $20260706+17i$ for prompt index $i$. Cross-prompt inputs cycle the trace of donor index $(i\bmod30)+1$ to the target's trace length. Random padding has a separately recorded transformation seed, and its payload overhead is calculated from the actual saved padding realization. The current campaign reuses these frozen seeds and vectors. The control and defense baseline reconstructions use distinct decoder seeds, so even their identical undefended length traces can yield different mean scores.

\subsection{Repeated Decoder Seeds and Adaptive Attacker}\label{sec:robustness-methods}
The primary seven-model table uses one assembled reconstruction per sample, selecting candidates from three stochastic samples per segment. Decoder-seed uncertainty and a defense-aware attacker were evaluated using direct logged lengths from the 512-token runs of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Two prompts were taken from each of the 15 topics ($n=30$ test prompts per model); the remaining 270 traces formed a calibration set with no identical test prompt. All 30 test scenarios nevertheless occur in 120 calibration rows under the other four wording templates. The test uses the first wording template throughout. Thus the split is exact-prompt-disjoint, but not scenario-disjoint. An EOS-complete-only split would have left 137 (Qwen) and 167 (Llama) calibration traces, below the configured minimum of 200, and was therefore not used. Of the 30 test prompts, 24 (Qwen) and 21 (Llama) were EOS-complete.

Reconstruction was repeated for five decoder seed indices, \texttt{20260706}--\texttt{20260710}. For a given prompt the same decoder seed was used in every defense and attacker condition (common random numbers). The seed is derived by BLAKE2s over the tuple consisting of \texttt{decoder}, the seed index, and the prompt index and does not depend on \texttt{PYTHONHASHSEED}. Mean $\phi$ is reported with a crossed bootstrap 95\% confidence interval that resamples the prompt axis and the seed axis independently ($B=10{,}000$). Between-seed variation is the sample standard deviation of the five seed-specific means; seed repeats are not treated as independent prompts. These reconstructions use one stochastic T5 sample for the first heuristic segment per seed, scored against the full stored response. The primary table instead reconstructs every available segment with three samples per segment; its scores and the MAP scores therefore have different reconstruction scopes.

The adaptive attacker applies a local empirical MAP length inverse before the unchanged T5 decoder. Calibration uses the 270 traces of the same victim model with no exact test prompt. The inverse selects a modal length conditional on an observed value, or a modal length tuple conditional on a batch sum and group size; unseen observations and the final batch use explicit fallback and size-selection heuristics (Appendix~\ref{app:map-seeds}). This is not a global MAP estimate of the response's entire length sequence. This is a calibrated length inverse, not end-to-end T5 retraining. Baseline traces are scored with the frozen reconstructor only. Each of the five transformations is scored with both the frozen reconstructor and the MAP inverse. The design comprises 22 complete cells, each with 30 prompts and five decoder seeds. Source vectors, inverse inputs, and decoder seeds are retained; the complete experiment contains 3300 reconstructions.

The recorded encoder inputs quantify first-segment coverage (Table~\ref{tab:encoder_coverage}). Baseline first segments have median lengths of 14.5 observed units for Qwen and 16 for Llama, all retained. For \texttt{bucket\_8}, median segments contain 451.5 and 441.5 units, but only the first 64 units fit after the instruction prefix. For \texttt{pad\_32}, 32 units fit because each length value occupies two T5 tokens. With MAP inversion, all 30 prompt-indexed inputs per model remain truncated for bucketing and fixed padding; counts are one per model for \texttt{batch\_2}, zero for \texttt{batch\_4}, and eight (Qwen) and two (Llama) for \texttt{rand\_pad\_8}. Five decoder seeds reuse each condition's input. Actual saved token IDs were checked against the pinned tokenizer. The comparisons therefore combine changes in length values, segmentation, encoded representation, and retained prefix; observation coverage is not held constant.

\section{Results}
The completed EOS-selector campaign comprises 2100 reconstructions from validated archived length traces, 600 reconstructions of separately collected TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini responses, 240 trace-control reconstructions, 360 defense-pilot reconstructions and 3300 MAP reconstructions. Every planned sample is present and independently checked. The primary and verified-reference cohorts request all available heuristic segments; the 96-token pilots allow up to three, and the 512-token MAP experiment reconstructs one. These differences preclude interpreting cross-experiment score differences as victim-budget effects alone.

\subsection{Effects of Framing Transformations on the Frozen Reconstructor}\label{sec:defense}
All five framing transformations reduce mean $\phi$ relative to their defense-pilot baseline (Table~\ref{tab:defense_results}), and all ten paired comparisons are significant after Holm correction. Reductions range from 43.8\% for \texttt{pad\_32} on Qwen to 89.6\% for \texttt{bucket\_8} on Qwen. Batching reduces scores by 70.9--79.3\% and changes the number of observable units from 96 to 48 or 24. Figure~\ref{fig:defense_tradeoff} relates these changes to payload overhead calculated from the actual saved transformation vectors.

These results describe the complete frozen pipeline, including changes in heuristic segmentation and encoder truncation after each transformation. They do not isolate information removed by a defense. In all 60 \texttt{pad\_32} pilot records, the retained encoder input is identical and reconstruction makes one decoder call. An extended audit covering these records and all 600 \texttt{pad\_32} records in the five-seed study finds one unique retained input within each suite, model and attacker group. Although the full 512-token-budget traces differ in their number of positions, that variation is removed before decoding by the 80-token encoder limit. The positive lexical scores therefore cannot demonstrate this decoder's use of response-specific trace-length variation. They reflect overlap between the references and outputs generated from the constant retained input under the recorded seeds; another attacker could still use information in the complete observable trace.

\subsection{Decoder-Seed Stability and Adaptation with a MAP Inverse}\label{sec:robustness}
To assess decoder-seed sensitivity, each condition was repeated with five seeds. Table~\ref{tab:robustness} reports all 22 model--defense--attacker settings. Each setting contains 30 prompts evaluated with five seeds, giving 150 reconstructions. The same seed was used across conditions for a given prompt. The 512-token victim budget defines the available trace, but only the first heuristic segment was submitted for reconstruction; its output was scored against the full stored response, not a first-sentence reference.

For the unmodified baseline traces, mean $\phi$ for the first-segment output against the full reference was 0.1988 for Qwen2.5-1.5B and 0.1860 for Llama-3.2-3B. Across all settings, the standard deviation of the five seed-specific means ranged from 0.0032 to 0.0184. Within these two-model, 30-prompt comparisons, variation over the five seeds was smaller than the observed frozen-pipeline score changes. This finding does not address victim-generation variability or validate equal input coverage across transformations.

When no defense-specific adaptation was applied, \texttt{bucket\_8} produced the largest reductions in mean $\phi$: 78.8\% for Qwen and 69.4\% for Llama. Batching reduced the score by 36.8--49.5\%, while randomized padding reduced it by 37.5\% for Qwen and 43.8\% for Llama. Fixed-width \texttt{pad\_32} instead had a higher mean score than baseline, corresponding to signed reductions of $-19.2\%$ and $-22.1\%$. A negative reduction denotes increased lexical overlap. The frozen \texttt{pad\_32} encoder input is identical across both models and all 300 reconstructions; all 150 cross-model pairs with common prompt and seed indices produce identical predictions. Their different mean scores arise from scoring those predictions against different references. Response-specific trace-length variation does not reach this decoder.

The pattern changed after the prompt-disjoint MAP inverse was added. It was fitted on 270 separate calibration prompts per model and used to estimate original lengths before the unchanged T5 decoder was applied. Calibration and test prompts are exact-text disjoint but share scenario templates, so this evaluates new prompt variants within the represented scenarios. For \texttt{bucket\_8}, the adaptive mean scores reached 0.2167 for Qwen and 0.2075 for Llama, meeting or exceeding their frozen baselines. Adaptive \texttt{batch\_2} also exceeded baseline for both models. With \texttt{batch\_4}, the adaptive score was close to the Qwen baseline (a signed reduction of 2.8\%) and above the Llama baseline (a signed reduction of $-53.4\%$).

Adaptation had a different effect on the remaining transformations. For \texttt{pad\_32}, the MAP inverse also yields a constant retained input within each model. Applying it reduced mean $\phi$ to 0.1583 for Qwen and 0.1492 for Llama, which corresponds to reductions of 20.4\% and 19.7\% relative to baseline. For \texttt{rand\_pad\_8}, part of the score lost by the frozen reconstructor was recovered, although the adaptive scores remained below baseline by 8.8\% and 19.3\%, respectively.

All 3300 MAP reconstructions are present in the current campaign, and independent checks reproduce their per-record metrics and crossed-bootstrap intervals. A defense-aware inverse recovers much of the lexical score lost under bucketing and batching, but inversion also changes the sequence subsequently segmented and truncated by T5. With 80-token limits on both input and output, equal victim-token budgets do not imply equal encoder coverage or comparable sentence boundaries. The adaptive procedure uses the frozen, prompt-disjoint empirical length inverse followed by the unchanged T5 decoder, without end-to-end retraining on defended traces. Its scores therefore characterize this pipeline under limited adaptation; they do not isolate a defense's information loss or establish the performance of a fully retrained attacker.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Evidence of Trace-Dependent Recovery}
Shuffling and constant replacement reduce lexical scores for both models after Holm correction. Neither cross-prompt contrast is conclusive under the adjusted signed-rank test. Thus the experiments support sensitivity to selected trace features, not a universal advantage of the target trace over every partial ablation. Controls preserve response-dependent quantities such as length, mean or histogram and can change segmentation. Their residual scores cannot be assigned entirely to the decoder's language prior. Estimating information-theoretic leakage would require a defined response variable, repeated sampling and a suitable estimator.

\subsection{Limits of the Cross-Model Comparison}
Exact logged byte lengths and saved input vectors establish the observations used by the reconstructor. Reference quality remains a distinct issue: archived TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini fragments have malformed word boundaries, whereas the new two-model cohorts pass explicit full-sequence decoding checks. For fixed saved token IDs, legacy decoding reproduces the archived inputs and references exactly, while full-sequence decoding restores word-boundary spaces. With reconstruction settings and per-sample seeds held constant, both resulting score increases have paired scenario-bootstrap intervals excluding zero. This effect jointly includes the changed length trace and changed scoring reference; neither contribution is isolated. The archived low scores therefore cannot support a general conclusion of poor reconstructor transfer to these two models.

The five other archived-reference series support conditional comparisons of the fixed reconstructor on their emitted texts, not an intrinsic model-vulnerability ranking. Historical token IDs and exact victim revisions are unavailable, while tokenizer vocabulary, response style, refusal behavior and compatibility with T5 training data can differ. The matched two-model rendering experiment is consequently reported separately from those five series. The synthetic corpus also contains only 60 scenarios, and the MAP test and calibration sets share those scenarios despite exact-prompt disjointness.

\subsection{Practical Exploitability}
For the largest mean in the archived-reference cohort, $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$ is 76.00\% and ROUGE-1 precision is 0.3284. Neither quantity is a disclosure rate: lexical overlap does not verify an entity, proposition or actionable answer. No semantic annotation or task-specific disclosure evaluation was performed. Practical harm might involve recovered phrases, entities, refusals or topic cues, but the present metrics do not quantify those outcomes. Deployment-facing conclusions additionally require extraction of observables from encrypted traffic and validation under transport buffering and multiplexing.

Reconstruction performance, transport observability, and mitigation performance require separate evidence. The MAP inverse recovered score losses on bucketing and batching within the evaluated pipeline, but it also changes segmentation and the amount of input retained under the 80-token encoder limit. The study therefore measures the combined effect of the length transform, inverse, preprocessing, and fixed decoder. For \texttt{pad\_32}, the complete observable trace retains its number of positions, but the actual retained encoder input is constant within each condition. Its positive lexical scores are consequently not evidence that this decoder exploits response-specific length variation. This finding concerns $E(Z)$ and does not establish absence of information in $Z$ for other attackers. A comparison that fixes observation coverage across conditions would help isolate these mechanisms. End-to-end training on defended traces and disclosure-oriented human or task evaluations remain separate extensions.

\subsection{Limitations}
Candidate selection is verified through the first EOS, but its unnormalized sum of sampling-processed log-scores is still an implementation choice. Its preference across different candidate lengths is not a calibrated measure of correctness. The study evaluates one pair of pretrained T5 checkpoints with heuristic segmentation and fixed sampling settings; it does not compare ranking objectives or establish optimal reconstruction performance. The one-candidate MAP study has a different decoding scope and bypasses this selection rule.

The observation is an instrumented log oracle. No packet capture or experiment with TLS, HTTP/2 or QUIC demonstrates that its token boundaries are available to a passive observer. The two historical reference-format diagnostics remain in the archived cohort for traceability, and the other archived references lack original token-ID provenance. Separately collected references are verified for TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini, but their configurations are not certified replicas of the historical victim executions.

The primary cohorts use one assembled reconstruction per prompt with three stochastic samples per segment. Decoder-seed uncertainty is evaluated separately for two models and 30 prompts; five seeds are not five independent prompt samples. Scenario bootstrap accounts for grouping within the 60 represented scenarios, without establishing generalization beyond the synthetic corpus. MAP calibration and test inputs are disjoint as exact prompt strings, not as scenarios. Pilot decoder seeds are condition-specific, and their single realizations cannot separate all stochastic variation from transformation effects.

The main archived responses reach the token limit in 34.3--100\% of cases; both historical and new Phi-3.5-mini cohorts lack EOS-complete responses. Reconstruction attempts every heuristic segment in the primary and verified-reference cohorts, with two empty terminal outputs in the archived cohort; the 96-token pilots allow up to three segments and MAP uses one. All use the full stored response as the scoring reference. Transform-dependent segmentation and the 80-token encoder limit prevent treating these scopes as matched information budgets. The MAP inverse is not end-to-end retraining, and payload overhead excludes transport syntax, buffering delay and latency. Finally, the historical Gemma cohort uses a different victim-generation implementation from the other six archived cohorts.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
The study used synthetic prompts and locally generated responses; it did not intercept user traffic or process identifiable personal data. The artifact release is intended to support defensive evaluation of streaming implementations. Deployment testing should use authorized systems, controlled accounts, and coordinated disclosure when a service-specific weakness is found.

\section{Conclusions}
The completed campaign provides 6600 T5 reconstructions with EOS-bounded candidate selection and independent verification of the recorded candidates and metrics. Reconstruction from validated logged lengths yields mean lexical scores of 0.509--0.577 for five archived-reference cohorts. For separately collected TinyLlama and Phi-3.5-mini responses with verified full-sequence decoding, the means are 0.468 and 0.527. Legacy rendering of their saved token IDs exactly reproduces the corresponding archived references and length vectors. With both renderings reconstructed under matched T5 settings and seeds in this campaign, changing the input trace and scoring reference increases mean $\phi$ by 0.3845 and 0.3972. This computational comparison identifies observation and reference construction as consequential parts of the evaluation; it does not separate their individual contributions or establish an intrinsic model-vulnerability ranking.

The partial-ablation controls support sensitivity to selected trace features: four of six contrasts remain significant after Holm correction, comprising shuffling and constant replacement for both models; neither cross-prompt contrast is conclusive. In the 96-token pilots, all ten model--transformation contrasts show significant score reductions after correction, ranging from 43.8\% to 89.6\%. These reductions describe the tested frozen reconstructor and realized transformations. The first-segment, five-seed study shows that an exact-prompt-disjoint MAP length inverse can recover much of the loss under bucketing and batching, although calibration shares scenario templates with the test set.

Constant \texttt{pad\_32} encoder inputs show why positive lexical overlap need not demonstrate use of response-specific trace variation. Practical privacy claims require evidence beyond these lexical scores. Transform-dependent segmentation and the 80-token encoder limit change the retained input coverage, and payload-size accounting excludes transport costs and latency. Evaluations with matched observation coverage, unseen scenarios, extraction from encrypted traffic, and disclosure-oriented outcomes are needed to establish the deployment significance of these results.

\section{Local Length Inversion and Random Seeds}\label{app:map-seeds}

\subsection{Empirical Length Inverse}
Let $c(x)$ count individual clean lengths in the calibration corpus, and let $S=\{x:c(x)>0\}$. For an unbatched defense, the calibration pass counts $C_z(x)$, the occurrences of clean length $x$ aligned with defended value $z$. Deterministic transformations use one pass; \texttt{rand\_pad\_8} uses 16 independent padding realizations per calibration trace. The inverse returns the most frequent $x$ in $C_z$, resolving ties in favor of the smaller length. An unseen $z$ returns the global mode of $c$, with the same tie rule. Thus the estimator is local to an observed unit. The baseline bypasses inversion.

For batching with maximum size $k\in\{2,4\}$, calibration counts tuples in non-overlapping consecutive groups, including each final partial group. Let $C_{z,s}(\mathbf{x})$ count a tuple of size $s$ and sum $z$, and let $T(s)$ count calibration traces whose final group has size $s$ (including full final groups). Interior observed groups are assigned size $k$. For the last observed group, the implemented heuristic chooses
\[
 \widehat{s}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq s\leq k}
 \left\{\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}} C_{z,s}(\mathbf{x})\right]\max(1,T(s))\right\},
\]
with ties resolved in favor of the larger $s$. This product of counts is a size-selection heuristic, not a separately validated posterior for the final group.

For the chosen $(z,s)$, the inverse returns the most frequent recorded tuple, with lexicographically smallest tuples winning ties. If that pair was never observed, dynamic programming finds a tuple in $S^s$ with sum $z$ maximizing
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^{s}\log\widetilde p(x_j),
 \qquad
 \widetilde p(x)=\frac{c(x)+1}{\sum_{u\in S}c(u)+|S|}.
\]
The recurrence appends one supported length at a time, retains the best tuple for each attainable subtotal, and rejects subtotals exceeding $z$; equal floating-point scores use the lexicographic tie rule. If the requested sum is unattainable, it repeats the global mode $s$ times, which need not preserve $z$. These rules specify the implemented local inverse and do not constitute globally optimal sequence inference.

\subsection{Decoder, Transformation and Bootstrap Seeds}
Define $H(a_1,\ldots,a_m)$ by converting each argument with Python's \texttt{str}, joining the strings with byte \texttt{0x1f}, UTF-8 encoding, and applying BLAKE2s with a four-byte digest. Interpret the digest as an unsigned big-endian integer and apply bitwise AND with \texttt{0x7fffffff}. This is the artifact function \texttt{stable\_seed}; it is independent of Python's randomized \texttt{hash()}.

The immutable main and verified-reference inventories store each exact seed in the \texttt{reconstruction\_seed} field. The matched renderings use the same recorded seed for each prompt. For the two pilots, the decoder seed is
\[
H(\texttt{secondary-pilot-decoder-v1},20260706,\texttt{label},i,
  \texttt{suite},\texttt{condition}),
\]
where $i$ is the recorded prompt index and the suite is \texttt{trace\_control} or \texttt{defense\_pilot}. Thus the two pilot baselines do not share decoder seeds. The defense transformation seed is $H(\texttt{secondary-pilot-trace-v1},20260706,\texttt{label},i,\texttt{condition})$; shuffling instead uses $20260706+17i$.

For MAP, decoder seed $H(\texttt{decoder},r,i)$ is reused across models, defenses and attacker modes for a given prompt index and seed index $r\in\{20260706,\ldots,20260710\}$. Transformation seed $H(\texttt{trace},20260920,\texttt{label},i,\texttt{condition})$ fixes a trace realization across decoder repeats and attacker modes. The inverse is fitted with seed $a=H(20260920,\texttt{label},\texttt{condition})$; calibration trace index $j$ and padding draw $d$ use $H(\texttt{calibration},a,\texttt{condition},j,d)$, with zero-based indices.

The main and verified-reference aggregators initialize their NumPy generator with \texttt{20260706}, consuming it in the pinned aggregation order. For every pilot or MAP cell, the bootstrap generator instead uses $H(\texttt{bootstrap},\texttt{suite},\texttt{label},\texttt{condition},\texttt{attacker})$; its paired contrast with baseline uses the same arguments with \texttt{contrast} replacing \texttt{bootstrap}. Each generator is initialized separately. Pilot intervals resample 30 prompt scores; MAP intervals independently resample the 30 prompt indices and five seed indices, then average their Cartesian-product submatrix. All use 10,000 replicates and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The matched-rendering contrast separately uses seed \texttt{20261101} and 60 paired scenario means. Exact key strings, inventories and aggregation order are retained in the supplementary code and protocols.
