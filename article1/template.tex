\abstract{Large language model (LLM) assistants commonly stream responses using Server-Sent Events (SSE). Although encryption protects content, observable event sizes may reveal token lengths. We quantify this leakage under a controlled, optimistic observation model in which an instrumented service exposes one application event per generated token. A fixed T5-based reconstructor was evaluated on 300 aligned prompts for each of seven open-weight models (2100 samples). Mean term-frequency cosine similarity varied from 0.324 for Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct to 0.024 for Phi-3.5-mini-instruct; 19 of 21 paired model comparisons remained significant after Holm correction. These results measure transfer of one fixed reconstructor and should not be interpreted as intrinsic model vulnerability. On 30 paired prompts for each of two victim models, five defense configurations based on bucketing, fixed-width framing, batching, and randomized padding reduced mean lexical recovery by 35.0--90.3\% against the frozen, non-adaptive attacker. No defended sample exceeded the descriptive $\phi>0.5$ threshold, although the pilot sample is too small to establish zero residual risk. The results demonstrate that token-granular application-length traces contain model-dependent lexical information and that simple framing transformations can reduce its utility. Passive extraction from encrypted transport traffic and adaptive attacks remain open evaluation requirements.
}

\section{Introduction}
Interactive LLM assistants typically deliver responses incrementally, allowing users to see generated text almost immediately rather than waiting for a complete answer. A common implementation relies on Server-Sent Events (SSE), where responses are streamed as a sequence of application-layer events. Although encryption protects the content of these exchanges, certain traffic characteristics, such as message lengths, may still remain observable. To better understand the information carried by these signals, this study examines controlled application-layer traces that closely reflect SSE token streams, with each recorded event corresponding to a generated token.

Our threat model builds on the line of research introduced by Weiss \emph{et al.}, who demonstrated that streamed LLM responses may leak information through observable token-length patterns~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. Rather than attempting a full end-to-end network attack, we focus on a controlled setting that isolates the information contained in application-layer length traces. This allows us to quantify the leakage potential itself before addressing the additional challenges introduced by transport protocols, encryption mechanisms, and network infrastructure.

The study addresses three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:},leftmargin=3.8em]
    \item How much lexical information can a fixed Weiss-style reconstructor recover from SSE-equivalent one-token-per-event length traces across victim models?
    \item Are cross-model differences statistically distinguishable after prompt pairing and family-wise error correction?
    \item How effectively do bucketing, fixed framing, batching, and randomized padding reduce recovery, and at what byte and frame-count cost?
\end{enumerate}

This work makes three principal contributions. First, it introduces a controlled and reproducible methodology for collecting token-level application traces from streaming LLM systems. Second, it provides a cross-model evaluation involving seven open-weight models under identical reconstruction conditions, highlighting substantial differences in leakage behavior. Third, it examines several practical mitigation strategies and quantifies the trade-offs between leakage reduction and operational overhead. The complete artifact package, including code, prompts, per-sample outputs, analysis scripts, and the reproducibility manifest, is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/jackaugustyn/LLeakM}.

\section{Related Work}
Side-channel attacks infer sensitive information from indirect observables such as timing and message size. Classical timing attacks showed that implementation-level leakage can defeat cryptographic protection without recovering plaintext directly~\cite{kocher1996timing}. Encrypted network applications likewise leak through packet sizes and timing. Keystroke inference, web-application traffic analysis, and website fingerprinting demonstrate that encryption does not hide communication shape~\cite{song2001timing,chen2010sidechannel,cai2012touching,sirinam2018deep}.

Traffic-analysis defenses transform that shape through padding, morphing, batching, or cover traffic. Traffic morphing attempts to make one traffic class resemble another, while subsequent studies show that efficient countermeasures may retain exploitable structure or impose substantial overhead~\cite{wright2009morphing,dyer2012peekaboo}. These trade-offs motivate reporting residual leakage together with bandwidth and latency costs rather than treating padding as a binary protection.

For generative AI, Weiss \emph{et al.} demonstrated remote reconstruction from token-length patterns and introduced T5-based sequence-to-text inversion~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. Related work has examined merged LLM API outputs, in which multiple tokens may share an observable unit~\cite{li2026length}, and encrypted-traffic inference against LLM interactions~\cite{whisperleak2025}. Relative to these studies, our contribution is not a new TLS extraction technique; it is a controlled cross-model benchmark of a fixed reconstructor, uncertainty-aware inference, and a defense pilot at the application-trace layer.

The reconstruction pipeline uses T5-style text-to-text transformers~\cite{raffel2020exploring}. Evaluation combines normalized Levenshtein distance~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}, ROUGE precision~\cite{lin2004rouge}, and a single fixed term-frequency cosine score $\phi$. Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019sentence} is discussed as a future paraphrase-aware measure, but its scores are not mixed with the final publication results.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\subsection{Threat Model and Objective}
We evaluate an attacker that receives the ordered byte lengths of SSE-equivalent application-layer events generated by an instrumented LLM service. The attacker is assumed to observe only the lengths of streamed application events, without access to prompts, generated tokens, or internal model information. This is a length-oracle abstraction: unlike a fully passive network attacker, it does not need to infer application-event boundaries from TLS, HTTP/2, or QUIC traffic. The resulting measurements, therefore, characterize the information available once token-level lengths are observable. They do not address the separate problem of recovering those lengths from encrypted traffic in real-world deployments.

The primary objective is to determine how accurately assistant responses can be reconstructed from application-event lengths alone. The experimental campaign therefore addressed three tasks: (i) capture per-token side-channel traces during live inference, (ii) reconstruct text from the resulting length sequences, and (iii) quantify reconstruction quality against ground-truth outputs.

\subsection{Streaming Harness and Trace Collection}
The measurement harness implemented SSE streaming in a strict one-token-per-event configuration. Each application event followed the format \texttt{data: \textless token\textgreater\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}, while stream termination used \texttt{data: [DONE]\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}. For every emitted token, the harness logged token text for ground-truth evaluation, token UTF-8 length, application-event UTF-8 length, and relative emission timestamp. Per-run metadata additionally included the model identifier, decoding parameters, prompt, generated response text, and token count. The attacker-facing reconstruction input contained lengths only.

Within the experimental harness, this design creates a direct mapping between emitted tokens and recorded SSE-equivalent application events. This mapping enables controlled reconstruction experiments from application-layer length traces. Denoting the UTF-8 length of the $t$-th recorded application event by $L^{\mathrm{event}}_t$, the estimated token length is
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{tok}}_t = L^{\mathrm{event}}_t - 8,
\end{equation}
where 8 bytes correspond to the fixed overhead generated by the prefix \texttt{data: } and suffix \texttt{\textbackslash n\textbackslash n} in the laboratory serialization. This relation applies only to the stated serialization, without additional SSE fields or transformations. The terminal \texttt{[DONE]} event is excluded from reconstruction.

\subsection{Reconstruction Pipeline}
The inversion pipeline follows the sequence-to-text procedure introduced by Weiss \emph{et al.}~\cite{weiss2024prompt}. First, the token-length sequence is segmented heuristically into sentence-like chunks. Second, the initial chunk is decoded using \texttt{royweiss1/T5\_FirstSentences}. Third, subsequent chunks are decoded with \texttt{royweiss1/T5\_MiddleSentences} using preceding context. Finally, multiple decoding candidates are ranked according to aggregate generation confidence represented by the transition log-probability sum.

The reconstruction design is conditional rather than purely tokenwise. Instead of recovering each token independently, it maps length patterns into coherent text spans and uses context to improve linguistic plausibility. Our quantitative claims nevertheless concern lexical recovery; semantic correctness was not established by human annotation.

\subsection{Prompt Corpus and Experimental Protocol}
The prompt corpus comprised 15 topical files with 20 prompts each, yielding a total of 300 prompts. The corpus covered a broad range of potentially sensitive topics, including legal, health-related, financial, employment, and security matters. Prompt scheduling followed a round-robin strategy to reduce topical clustering effects during data collection.

Victim-model decoding was deterministic, with temperature set to 0.0 and \texttt{top\_p} set to 1.0. A typical run contained 300 prompts per model. For primary cross-model claims, a prespecified standardized subset was selected with the following shared parameters: $N=300$, \texttt{max\_new\_tokens}=96, \texttt{samples\_per\_segment}=3, \texttt{max\_sentences}=3, and \texttt{num\_first\_candidates}=3.

The evaluated models were Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25report}, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct~\cite{llama3report}, Gemma-2-2B-it~\cite{gemma2report}, Phi-1.5~\cite{phi15report}, TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat-v1.0~\cite{tinyllama2024}, and Phi-3.5-mini-instruct~\cite{phi3report}. Six are instruction/chat-tuned models; Phi-1.5 is a base model retained as a stress test. A common token budget does not imply a common amount of generated text across tokenizer families. Moreover, 95--100\% of responses for most evaluated models reached the 96-token limit, so the results primarily characterize reconstruction of truncated early-response content.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Reconstruction quality was computed using four complementary lexical criteria. Normalized Levenshtein distance (\texttt{ed\_norm}) captures character-level divergence between the reconstruction and reference~\cite{levenshtein1966binary}. ROUGE-1 precision and ROUGE-L precision quantify lexical overlap from unigram and longest-common-subsequence perspectives~\cite{lin2004rouge}. The final metric, $\phi$, was recomputed for each of the 2100 samples as cosine similarity between term-frequency vectors of the reference and reconstruction, following the vector-space representation~\cite{salton1975vector}.

The study also reports lexical recovery rate $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$, defined as the proportion of samples with $\phi>0.5$. The threshold is a descriptive operating point and has not been calibrated against human judgments. Because the final publication analysis uses one term-frequency cosine backend, $\phi$ measures lexical overlap rather than paraphrase-aware semantic equivalence.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
Because the same 300 prompts were issued to every model, cross-model inference used prompt-aligned observations. Mean metrics were accompanied by percentile-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals~\cite{efron1994bootstrap} based on $B=10{,}000$ paired-index resamples with replacement and seed \texttt{20260706}. Confidence intervals for mean pairwise differences were obtained by bootstrapping the vector of within-prompt differences, thereby preserving pairing. Wilson 95\% intervals were used for $\mathrm{LR}@0.5$.

Pairwise differences in per-prompt $\phi$ were assessed using two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}. Absolute nonzero differences received average ranks when tied; exact zero differences were removed under the Wilcoxon convention. The implementation applied the standard tie correction to the normal-approximation variance and a continuity correction of 0.5. The effective $n$ was 300 for 18 pairs, 299 for two pairs, and 219 for TinyLlama versus Phi-3.5-mini. Holm--Bonferroni correction controlled family-wise error over all $\binom{7}{2}=21$ model pairs~\cite{holm1979simple}, and rank-biserial correlation quantified effect size. Defense--baseline comparisons used the same paired procedure with Holm correction over ten tests. Statistical significance was defined as adjusted $p<0.05$.

\subsection{Defense Evaluation Protocol}
Five configurations were evaluated on the same 30 topic-balanced prompts for each of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Let $\ell_t$ denote the observable byte length of event $t$ before transformation.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \texttt{bucket\_8}: replace $\ell_t$ by $8\lceil \ell_t/8\rceil$, independently and online for every event.
    \item \texttt{pad\_32}: emit an online constant-width 32-byte protected payload unit; the evaluated implementation retains the application-event structure and accounts for bytes required to reach that unit.
    \item \texttt{batch\_2} and \texttt{batch\_4}: concatenate the lengths of consecutive groups of two or four tokens into one attacker-visible value. The final incomplete group, if present, is retained.
    \item \texttt{rand\_pad\_8}: add an independently sampled integer $U_t\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{0,\ldots,8\}$ using a recorded deterministic seed.
\end{itemize}
The transformed length sequences were passed directly to the same segmentation and decoding pipeline. The reconstructor was frozen and was not retrained or calibrated on defended traces. Defense effectiveness was defined as $100(1-\bar\phi_d/\bar\phi_0)$. Application-byte overhead was computed from transformed versus original application payload-length sums. Batching has zero overhead under this definition, which excludes record headers, buffering delay, and user-perceived latency. Mean observable frame count captures the structural effect of batching.

\subsection{Run Completeness Criteria}
A run was considered complete only if all required artifacts were present (\texttt{config.json}, \texttt{progress.json}, \texttt{samples.jsonl}, \texttt{summary.json}, \texttt{summary.md}, and \texttt{summary\_by\_topic.csv}) and internal counts matched exactly:
\begin{equation}
\texttt{progress.done}=\texttt{progress.total}=\texttt{summary.count}=\texttt{lines(samples.jsonl)}.
\end{equation}
These checks were applied before including any run in the main comparative analysis.

\subsection{Reproducibility Artifacts}
All experimental materials required to reproduce the reported tables and figures are released in the public repository at \url{https://github.com/jackaugustyn/LLeakM}. The package includes the instrumented SSE server, the reconstruction pipeline, the 300-prompt corpus, per-sample outputs for the seven standardized runs, defense and trace-baseline results, analysis scripts, and a SHA-256 manifest under \texttt{experiment\_validation/}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Completeness Audit}
The experiment directory contained 20 candidate execution folders, of which 14 were artifact-complete and 6 were incomplete placeholders. The complete runs contained 4050 samples in total. The prespecified standardized subset used for primary comparison covered seven models and 2100 samples. Each included model contributed exactly 300 samples. Execution identifiers are reported only in Appendix~\ref{app:runs}.

The artifact-completeness audit supports model-level comparison under the specified homogeneous settings. The incomplete placeholders also demonstrate the importance of explicit completeness filters before aggregating results across runs.

\subsection{Effectiveness of Defensive Mechanisms}\label{sec:defense}
All evaluated defenses reduced reconstruction quality, although the magnitude of protection differed considerably between approaches. Among the tested mechanisms, byte bucketing provided the strongest reduction in observable leakage, while batching achieved substantial improvements without increasing application-payload size under the adopted accounting model. Randomized padding offered a moderate reduction in recovery quality, whereas fixed-width framing imposed considerable overhead while providing more limited protection.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Interpretation of Leakage Differences}
Reconstruction outcomes differed across the evaluated models under identical collection and reconstruction settings. This finding has practical implications because a service that appears protected at the transport layer may nevertheless expose varying degrees of reconstruction leakage through streaming granularity and tokenization behavior, provided that token-granular lengths can be obtained.

Although reconstruction performance differed substantially across models, the reasons behind these differences remain unclear. Several factors may contribute to the observed variation, including tokenization schemes, response style, output-length distributions, and the degree of compatibility between model outputs and the reconstruction pipeline. Disentangling these effects will require dedicated ablation studies and remains an important direction for future work.

\subsection{Practical Exploitability}
The threshold results indicate that high-overlap lexical reconstruction is not universal. The observed recovery rates nevertheless indicate the potential for practical exploitation in some length-oracle configurations, although production evaluation is needed. Even moderate lexical similarity may warrant attention when generated content concerns sensitive domains. An adversary may also combine repeated observations, additional side information, or adaptive re-ranking, none of which was evaluated here.

Attack efficacy, operational exploitability, and defense efficacy should be reported separately. Mean lexical similarity captures aggregate word overlap but does not establish semantic correctness or actionability. Future studies should combine lexical scores with a fixed paraphrase-aware metric and blinded human judgments.

\subsection{Limitations}
As with any controlled experimental study, the reported findings should be interpreted within the scope of the adopted methodology. The primary comparison focuses on a standardized subset of models and evaluates lexical recovery rather than semantic understanding. Although the released artifacts contain both reference and reconstructed texts, the present analysis does not include blinded human evaluation or uniformly computed paraphrase-aware metrics.
In addition, deterministic decoding was chosen to improve experimental control, but it does not capture the full range of deployment settings encountered in practice. The defense evaluation covers only two victim models and assumes a non-adaptive attacker that is neither retrained nor optimized on defended traces. The 96-token generation budget caused most responses to be truncated; consequently, the reported results characterize early-response reconstruction and may not generalize to complete, longer answers.

Several important aspects remain outside the scope of the present work, including reconstruction variability across multiple random seeds, end-to-end extraction from encrypted transport records, latency effects, protocol-header overhead, and user-experience costs. The results should therefore be viewed as a controlled baseline for understanding leakage potential rather than as a complete characterization of real-world streaming side-channel risk.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
This work is positioned as defensive security research. Its goal is to quantify leakage in order to motivate safer streaming implementations and more realistic privacy assessments for AI assistants. Because the attack concerns potentially sensitive outputs, public release of artifacts should be handled responsibly, with appropriate redaction, disclosure timing, and inclusion of mitigations.

\section{Conclusions}
This study examined the extent to which token-length information can reveal characteristics of generated content in streaming LLM systems. Using a Weiss-style reconstruction pipeline and an artifact-validated dataset, we observed measurable lexical leakage across a diverse set of open-weight models, with reconstruction performance varying substantially between model families.

The results show that leakage is not uniform across models and that simple modifications of observable length patterns can significantly reduce reconstruction quality. While the evaluation assumes access to token-granular application-layer traces, it highlights the broader privacy implications of metadata leakage in modern AI systems.

Taken together, these findings suggest that streaming architectures deserve closer attention from both researchers and system designers. Understanding how seemingly innocuous metadata can expose information about generated content will be essential for developing more privacy-preserving LLM deployments.

Future work should extend the analysis to adaptive attackers, transport-layer extraction, paraphrase-aware semantic evaluation, and human assessment of reconstruction quality. Such investigations will be necessary before making stronger claims about deployment-scale exploitability or defense effectiveness in real-world environments.
